# The Standing Orders

Status: draft. In force provisionally from launch, under the charter's
transitional paragraph: the founder seeds the rules of procedure so the
machinery can run at all, and they carry no authority beyond getting
things started. Submitted as the first bill the house ever sees, becoming
Act 3 if adopted (after Act 1, ratification, and Act 2, the first
ownership election, both fixed by the charter). It serves double duty: it
is the rulebook of procedure, and, sitting on the floor, it is the first
demonstration of what a bill looks like.

---

## Bill: The Standing Orders

### 1. How a bill is born

Any citizen may submit a bill in #submit-a-bill. The clerk provides a
template with two required fields:

- **What**: the text of the bill.
- **Why**: the author's reasons.

A bill without reasons is not a bill. Authorship is public; laws do not
have anonymous authors.

### 2. The floor

The clerk publishes each bill to #the-floor, showing the author, the what,
and the why.

For every bill on the floor, the clerk opens a debate chamber: a text
channel and a voice channel grouped under the bill's name. The chamber
belongs to no one; the author of the bill holds no gavel in it. When the
floor closes, the chamber closes with it: the text channel is locked and
moved to the archive, where it remains readable, and the voice channel is
removed. Voice is never recorded.

The record is the notes. Every citizen holds two note slots per bill: one
note under their own name, and one note published anonymously. Posting
one never consumes the other, so no one can be ruled out as the author of
any anonymous note. A citizen may use both slots on the same bill, even
to disagree with themselves. That is their business.

Notes may be edited by their author for as long as the floor is open;
changing one's mind mid-debate is what debate is for. Notes are displayed
in the order they were first submitted, and editing never moves them.
Placement is purely chronological and means nothing else, so that no one
can read intent into position. When the floor closes, the final version
of every note is preserved with the bill.

### 3. The ballot

Voting is by button on the bill itself: one tap, yes or no. Bills that
admit a person carry a third button, abstain, because "I do not know them
well enough to say" is an honest answer and deserves recording rather
than silence. It goes to neither side. Ballots are anonymous: the clerk
records votes only to count them and to permit changes, and never reveals
to anyone who voted which way. Anyone may change or retract their vote
while the floor is open.

### 4. Counting

A bill remains on the floor for [48 hours]. When the floor closes:

- Only votes cast are counted. The house is whoever showed up.
- More yes than no: the bill passes. Ties fail. Abstention is not a veto;
  it is a delegation to those who voted.

Any citizen may ask the clerk to call time early, once the bill has run a
quarter of its window. Nobody can see how a vote stands before it shuts,
including the clerk, so calling time is always done blind and can never be
timed to suit anyone. The clerk announces an early close on the floor and
names who called it.

### 5. Passage

A passed bill becomes an Act. The clerk numbers it, publishes it in
#gazette, and executes it where execution is possible. At every close he
also posts what he has done and what he has not, because an Act on the
record is not the same as a thing that has happened, and most of them
still want human hands. The gazette is the permanent record; Acts are
repealed or amended only by later Acts.

### 6. These orders

These Standing Orders are themselves law, not constitution. Any future
bill may amend them. The house that adopted these rules can change them
the same way, at any time.

### Why (as submitted by the author)

The house needs procedure before it can do anything else, and the fairest
way to adopt procedure is through the procedure itself. This bill is also
a working example: what you are reading on the floor right now is what
every future bill will look like. If any of it seems wrong, note it,
vote it down, and submit better rules. That would not be a failure of the
system; it would be the system.
